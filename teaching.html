Teaching

I approach economics as a way of understanding the world rather than simply a collection of models and methods. My teaching connects economic ideas with data, institutions, history, social and cultural norms, and real-world problems, while encouraging students to become active participants in the process of inquiry.

Courses

My teaching spans economics and business curricula, from introductory courses to advanced empirical and interdisciplinary work. Courses I have taught include:

Economic Development
Development, institutions, inequality, globalization, and the experiences of developing and transitional economies.

Behavioral Economics & Finance
How psychological, social, and institutional factors shape economic and financial decision-making.

Econometrics
Empirical methods for asking and answering economic questions using real-world data.

Microeconomics & Macroeconomics
Foundational economic reasoning applied to individual choices, markets, institutions, growth, and the broader economy.

Data Analytics & AI
Applied economic analysis combining real-world datasets with emerging AI-supported approaches to research and problem solving.

02

Learning Beyond the Classroom

Some of the most meaningful learning occurs when students use economics outside the boundaries of a traditional assignment. I incorporate experiential and community-engaged projects that connect students with organizations, real datasets, and questions that matter to the communities around them.

These experiences ask students not simply to complete an exercise, but to work as researchers and problem solvers: defining questions, analyzing evidence, communicating findings, and learning how economic reasoning operates in complex real-world settings.

03

AI & Data-Driven Learning

I have increasingly incorporated generative AI into data-intensive economics education. Rather than treating AI simply as a productivity tool, I use it as a scaffold that can help students move beyond technical barriers and engage more deeply with empirical questions.

With thoughtful guidance, students who have relatively limited quantitative training can begin working with sophisticated datasets, explore economic questions, evaluate evidence, and communicate their findings. At the same time, students learn to question AI-generated results, recognize model limitations, and use these tools responsibly.

04

Student Research & Mentoring

Mentoring student research has been a central part of my teaching. I have worked with students on independent studies, collaborative research, applied data projects, and interdisciplinary questions that allow them to pursue their own intellectual interests.

My goal is to help students develop the confidence to formulate meaningful questions, work independently with evidence, and see themselves as contributors to knowledge rather than simply consumers of it.
